SightLine

A medicine label, read aloud — on your phone, with no network.

It says the label back in Cantonese — a first build that speaks only what it can translate and refuses the rest. Every accuracy number in this deck was measured on the English read.

The moment we built this for

She is 78. The box of medicine is in her hand, and she cannot read it.

Two things stop her at once: her eyes no longer make out the small print, and what is printed on that box is English she never learned.

So the dose gets guessed — or it waits for a neighbour who is not coming today.

No statistic on this slide. It is the reason the next one matters.

The problem at scale
199,600 people

report long-term seeing difficulty in Hong Kong — 2.7% of the population, three in four of them 65 or over, and 188,569 people over 65 live alone.

The second barrier compounds the first: the label names the drug, the strength and the directions in English. An app that only magnifies the print leaves the language problem standing; one that only translates assumes she can see the text.

Every cloud reader on the market asks them to upload that label first. For medication, offline is not a feature — it is the only answer that holds.

The solution

Photograph the medicine label. It reads it aloud, names the drug, the strength and the dose — and refuses what it cannot read.

Our own recogniser: 942,166 parameters, 37 symbols (0-9, A-Z, space). No OCR library, no cloud, no account.

26.7 MB of ONNX on the device. Airplane mode: nothing about their medication leaves the phone.

Cantonese ships, a first build: drug name from an 18,839-pair licensed zh/en lexicon, dose from the read text, directions from a closed phrase set — and a refusal for a name it cannot translate. WebKit 14/14.

Every accuracy number is the English read. On 150 real medicine labels the name landed verbatim 8× — a floor (Taiwan, not HK).

Hard part 1 — inference in a browser. ONNX Runtime Web, our own graph, 26.7 MB shipped to the phone. No OCR library, no server, no fallback engine.

Hard part 2 — the refusal threshold. A field below confidence is omitted, never guessed: 10 of the 24 ground-truth fields on our bench are missing rather than wrong (current fixtures; 15 of 24 on the previous generation).

Why Cantonese needed no Chinese OCR. HK medicine labels print the drug name, strength and directions in English — the 0-9 A-Z alphabet we already read. The gap was only the voice (zh-HK) and a licensed zh/en lexicon, so the remaining risk is a wrong match, not a missing alphabet: an unknown name is refused, never guessed.

What we got wrong — and fixed

Four train/serve contract defects. We found them in our own code, measured them, and gated them.

  1. 1 · the graph was baked at 256 px; training crops are clamped to 320. The app fed the model the first 256 px of 98.8% of lines. 3.6% → 22.4% word accuracy; 200/200 crops changed their read.
  2. 2 · padding is not inert in a bidirectional CRNN. The backward pass resumes at the right edge, inside the pad, so zeros moved the hidden states across valid frames. 30.5% vs 19.0% crop-exact; CER 0.198 vs 0.308.
  3. 3 · the candidate list replaced the preprocessed image. Only CLAHE arms were offered, and raw deskewed grey measured best. 26.9% vs 23.9% word accuracy — the best input was unreachable.
  4. 4 · the deskew sign was inverted. We rotated by the estimate and doubled the tilt instead of removing it. 4.5° applied read back as 6.5–9.0°; it zeroed the two marginal off-axis pages.
Each defect now has a gate that fails if it returns: test_graph_width_contract.py · test_padding_invariant.py · crop_contract.js · deskew_verify.js.

Impact and policy

take five or more medicines at once — the highest of Hong Kong, Taiwan, South Korea, the UK and Australia (2016), and rising 2.7% a year. Polypharmacy plus sight loss is our use case.
